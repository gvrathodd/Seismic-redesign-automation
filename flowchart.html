Seismic redesign of beams, columns and joints

IS 13920:2016 (with Amendments 1 & 2) · procedure follows IITK-GSDMA EQ22, Example 5 (interior beam-column joint, Zone V)

Design step Code check Revision (loop back) Input / output

Goal of the flow (capacity design): (1) beams must yield in flexure before they fail in shear → Steps 2–3; (2) beams must yield before columns and joints → Steps 5–7. Every check uses the provided steel, so any revision loops back and re-runs the checks after it.

Avoiding over-design

Packaged software (STAAD Pro, ETABS etc.) runs these checks automatically but tends to over-design. In seismic design extra steel is not automatically safe. Extra beam steel raises the beam hinge moment, and everything after it (beam shear, the 1.4 column check, column shear, joint shear) is designed from that moment. Over-designed beams therefore lead to heavier columns and bigger joints. This procedure keeps every code factor but removes the usual simplifications:

Usual simplificationThis procedure
Each beam face designed as singly reinforced, then bottom steel raised to ½ top (Cl 6.2.3)The mandatory bottom steel is used as compression steel (doubly reinforced section), giving the least top + bottom steel
Capacities read from SP 16 tables or charts at the nearest lower value; bars rounded upCapacities by strain compatibility (IS 456 Cl 38.1, 39) for the bars actually chosen; bar combinations picked to just exceed the requirement
Column moment capacity at Pu = 0 for the strong column checkLeast capacity over the actual earthquake combinations, as Cl 7.2.1 requires. This is also safer for heavily loaded columns.
Members grouped and designed for the worst member in the groupEach joint designed for its own forces

Why each step exists

StepClauseWhat it protects against
1–2 Beam geometry & flexure6.1, 6.2Beam is a ductile fuse: enough steel to carry demand, but capped at 2.5% so it yields gradually instead of crushing.
3 Capacity-design shear6.3.3–6.3.5Flexure before shear. Links are designed for the shear that exists when both ends have already formed plastic hinges (1.4 × capacity), with concrete ignored, so shear can never govern.
4 Column checks7.1Axial stress ≤ 0.40 fck keeps the column ductile; size rules give room for beam-bar anchorage.
5 Strong column - weak beam7.2.1Beams before columns. Columns at each joint must be 1.4× stronger than the beams, so hinges form in beams, not columns (avoids storey collapse).
6 Column shear7.5Column must survive the shear created when beam hinges form.
7 Joint shear9.1Joint must carry the forces from beam bars at 1.25 fy (over-strength). This is the core of EQ22 Example 5 and is often the check that fails.
8 Confinement & detailing7.4, 7.6, 9.2Confined concrete at hinge zones and joints so they can rotate without crushing.

Notes

  • EQ22 was written against a draft of IS 13920. Where they differ, this flowchart uses the 2016 code: strong column ratio is 1.4 (EQ22 used 1.1); joint strength is in terms of √fck per Amendment 2.
  • EQ22 Example 5 revised the members (column 400×500 → 600×600, beam 300×500 → 300×600) without re-analysis. The last decision box above flags when re-analysis is advisable.
  • This flow is for an interior joint (Example 5). Exterior and roof joints (Examples 6–8) change only the joint confinement factor, the joint shear expression and the roof waiver in Step 5.
